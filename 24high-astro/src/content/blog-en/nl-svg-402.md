In this article, you will discover the history of cannabis: from the first archaeological finds and traditional medicine to modern science and global legalisation today.</p>
<h2>The botanical and geographical origins of cannabis</h2>
<p>Cannabis belongs to the Cannabaceae family and consists of several species, including<em>Cannabis sativa</em>,<em>Cannabis indica</em><span> and </span><em>Cannabis ruderalis</em>. Scientific research shows that cannabis originated in Central Asia, probably in regions that are now part of Mongolia, northern China and southern Siberia. Genetic studies show that this region is where cannabis first grew as a wild plant. Later, it was domesticated by humans, according to the <span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1525/j.ctt3fh2f8" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">2013 book 'Cannabis: Evolution and Ethnobotany' by Robert C. Clarke and Mark D. Merlin</a></span>.</p>
<h2>History of cannabis: Oldest use and archaeological evidence</h2>
<p>Based on archaeological findings, experts suspect that human use of cannabis dates back some 10 thousand years. Back then, this plant was not yet used as a recreational drug, but rather in the form of hemp fibres for making clothes, ropes and baskets. Excavations in China show that cannabis was grown and used as early as 4000 BC. Around this time, it appears that the plant was probably used not only for its fibre, but also for medicinal purposes, according to this <span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/4253540" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">study by Hui-Lin Li</a></span>.</p>
<h3>Where did cannabis come from</h3>
<p>Excavations have revealed not only hemp remains, but also seeds that were deliberately preserved or burnt. This suggests that cannabis was used early on, both practically (fibre) and symbolically or ritually. Archaeologists stress that the finds in central China are probably among the oldest examples of plant domestication worldwide.</p>
<p><img alt="history of cannabis" src="../../../../../files/index.html@id=2722.jpg&amp;file=hempfield.jpg" style="width: 1100px; max-width: 100%;"/></p>
<h2>Cannabis in the traditional medicine of China and other cultures</h2>
<p>Cannabis has thus been used in <span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="../../../../331/traditional-chinese-medicine-what-is-it-and-what-are-the-best-remedies.html" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">traditional Chinese medicine</a></span> for centuries. For example, the famous book Shennong Bencao Jing (around 2700 BC) describes cannabis as a medicinal plant against various ailments such as rheumatism, constipation and malaria. Modern scientific studies show that cannabis does indeed contain substances that have an effect on the human body's endocannabinoid system. According to <span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17712811/" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">this research by Ethan B. Russo</a></span>, this plant<span> could </span>therefore promote pain relief, digestive regulation and anti-inflammation.</p>
<p>It is important to stress that medical applications in ancient times were often based on observations and cultural interpretations, and were not always scientifically based as they are today. Modern studies do confirm some of these traditional claims, but also show that cannabis is not effective or safe for every condition. Therefore, its use in medicine remains the subject of intense debate and research.</p>
<h2>Spread through trade and culture</h2>
<p>Although cannabis has its origins in Asia, the plant is now used almost all over the world. This has actually been happening for a very long time, mainly because cannabis entered other parts of the world through trade routes in the past. This happened for instance via the Silk Road, which is of course a famous road travelled by many traders centuries ago. Cannabis thus also ended up in India, where it acquired religious significance. Within Hinduism, for example, the use of a cannabis drink called bhang is a tradition.</p>
<p>In the Arab world, cannabis was researched and described by medical scholars in the Middle Ages. For example, the Persian physician Avicenna, known for his work Canon of Medicine, mentioned cannabis as an analgesic, writes Ernest L. Abel in his book <span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4899-2189-5" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">Marihuana: The First Twelve Thousand Years</a></span>. Later, cannabis also reached Europe.</p>
<h2>Scientific breakthroughs: how THC and CBD changed medical research</h2>
<p>Attention to cannabis as medicine increased worldwide in recent decades. Consequently, scientists have done a lot of research on this plant. They found out that cannabinoids, such as THC and CBD, can help treat chronic pain, epilepsy and other conditions. This is what <span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2338251" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">Penny F. Whiting et al</a></span> wrote<span style="text-decoration: underline; color: #0907f5;"><a href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2338251" style="color: #0907f5; text-decoration: underline;">about in a 2015 study</a></span>. Still, we should mention that cannabis is not necessarily accepted as a standard medicine. You won't soon see a doctor prescribing it to a patient, although there are an increasing number of countries where medicinal cannabis can be used.</p>
<p><img alt="cannabis usage" src="../../../../../files/index.html@id=2721.jpg&amp;file=sweqq.jpg" style="width: 1100px; max-width: 100%;"/></p>
<h2>How cannabis works in the body - and what the risks are</h2>
<p>Cannabis affects the endocannabinoid system (ECS), a network of receptors such as CB1 (mainly in the brain) and CB2 (in the immune system), which are involved in pain perception, mood and appetite, among other things. The active ingredients THC and CBD bind to these receptors, leading to both therapeutic and psychoactive effects.</p>
<p>Although studies show positive effects in chronic pain and epilepsy, among others, experts also point out risks. Long-term recreational use of THC-rich cannabis can lead to dependence, anxiety disorders or depression, especially in young people or those with mental vulnerability.</p>

<h2>From coffee shops to legalisation: recreational use of cannabis worldwide</h2>
<p>Many people who use cannabis do not necessarily do so because of its medicinal properties. Recreational use of cannabis still seems to be much greater. It is not allowed in many countries, although there are somewhere cannabis is regulated. The Netherlands is, of course, the best-known example of this. Below, we list a few more countries where you can currently legally use cannabis recreationally:</p>
<ol>
<li>Canada</li>
<li>Uruguay</li>
<li>Luxembourg</li>
<li>Germany</li>
<li>Thailand</li>
</ol>
<p>Do pay attention to local laws, as the rules are slightly different in almost all countries.</p>
<p>The origins of cannabis thus go back to prehistoric times in Central Asia. What began as a versatile fibre plant grew into a global phenomenon with cultural, religious, medicinal and recreational uses. Over the centuries, the perception of cannabis kept changing: from sacred plant and medicine to banned drug. It has since been re-regulated in several countries.</p>
